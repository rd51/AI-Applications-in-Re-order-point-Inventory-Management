Why each stage overreacts

No one in the chain is being irrational. Each planner makes a sensible local decision, but they only see the orders arriving from the stage below, not what customers actually bought. Four things then stack up:

Forecasting off orders, not sales
A retailer sees sales jump 10 units and raises both its forecast and its safety stock, so it orders, say, 30 extra. The distributor sees +30, reads it as a trend, and orders +70.
Lead times
If restocking takes weeks, you must cover all those weeks of demand plus a buffer. Any change in forecast is multiplied by the lead time.
Order batching
Ordering in big lumps (full trucks, monthly POs) turns smooth demand into spikes and silences.
Price promotions and shortage gaming
Discounts make buyers stock up early. When supply is rationed, buyers inflate orders to get a bigger share, then cancel.

Reading the whiteboard

Your lecturer’s sketch plots demand against weeks 1 to 7 for three parties. The lowest, gentlest line is the customer. The lines above it start later and climb higher with sharper zigzags. That’s the bullwhip: each stage reacts later (lead time) and harder (amplified orders).

The dashed staircases show the levels each stage commits to, such as stock targets or capacity. Each one steps up later and higher than the one below, because each stage only finds out about the change once the order from downstream reaches it.

The title, “Single Source of Truth,” is the cure. If everyone plans from the same customer sales data, no one has to guess from inflated orders. Flip that switch above to see the upstream lines collapse back toward the red one.

What the lab asks you to build

  1. Simulation environment. Stages from manufacturer to customer, each with lead time, inventory and an ordering rule.
  2. Base simulation, no AI. Generate customer demand, let each stage order, and measure how the swing grows upstream. That’s what the chart shows with both switches off.
  3. AI forecasting. Give the retailer a model trained on historical demand so it orders from a cleaner signal.
  4. AI routing or optimisation. Use reinforcement learning or an optimiser to decide order quantities or how stock is allocated across the network.

The simulation uses an order-up-to policy: each stage orders what it was asked for plus (lead time + 1) × the change in its exponentially smoothed forecast.
